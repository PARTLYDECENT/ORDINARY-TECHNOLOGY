The Architecture of Inference: A Comprehensive Analysis of Non-Human Agency and Cognitive Amplification in Human-AI InteractionThe integration of artificial intelligence into the sphere of human cognition represents an unprecedented ontological shift in the history of technology and epistemology. Historically, the concept of "thinking by proxy" was considered a philosophical absurdity. Early phenomenological critiques, grounded in integrationist linguistics and embodied cognition, argued that one cannot outsource intrinsic mental states to an external object. Just as wearing a black tie at a funeral does not perform the act of mourning on the wearer's behalf, it was long maintained that a machine could not perform the act of thinking on behalf of a human. This perspective was echoed in early twentieth-century physiological literature, which cautioned that the powers of knowledge could never truly mature for an individual who attempted to do their thinking by proxy, positing that human cognitive faculties required direct, unmediated engagement with reality to function optimally.However, the advent of agentic artificial intelligence and generative foundation models has fundamentally fractured this historical paradigm. Through non-invasive human-AI interactions, artificial systems have begun to exhibit distinctly non-human levels of agency. These systems no longer function as passive repositories of information or simple deterministic calculators; rather, they serve as intelligent cognitive proxies that amplify human analytical capacity, strategic reasoning, and creative output. This phenomenon of "superagency"—a state where individuals, empowered by AI supertools, supercharge their creativity, productivity, and impact—is rapidly becoming the defining characteristic of modern knowledge work, holding a projected long-term economic potential of 4.4 trillion dollars in added productivity. By internalizing vast datasets and operating across high-dimensional latent spaces, these systems execute a form of cognition that is profoundly alien to human heuristic processing. They do not merely mimic human thought; they discover optimal topologies of strategy and design that human psychological limitations, biological constraints, and traditional heuristic biases actively obscure. The cognitive models driving these artificial intelligence systems—such as large-scale transformers, diffusion architectures, and generative adversarial networks—exhibit formal analogs of human cognitive functions.
